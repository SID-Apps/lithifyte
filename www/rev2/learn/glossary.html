Plain-English money glossary

Updated 9 August 2026 · 6 min read

Every term you are likely to meet on a statement, in a loan document or in a budgeting guide, defined plainly and without circularity. Definitions first, context second.

On this page

  • Interest and borrowing
  • Budgeting
  • Accounts and payments
  • Wealth

Interest and borrowing

APR

The annual percentage rate: the interest rate plus compulsory fees, expressed as one yearly figure so two credit products can be compared honestly. A low rate with a large arrangement fee can carry a higher APR than a higher rate with none.

AER

The annual equivalent rate, used for savings. It states what you would earn over a year once compounding is accounted for, so an account paying monthly can be compared fairly with one paying annually.

Amortisation

The process of paying off a debt through regular payments that cover both interest and principal. Early payments are mostly interest; later ones are mostly principal, which is why a mortgage balance falls slowly at first.

Principal

The amount actually borrowed, as distinct from the interest charged on it. Only payments that reduce principal reduce the debt.

Compound interest

Interest charged or earned on interest already added, rather than only on the original amount. It makes debts grow at an accelerating rate and long-term savings grow the same way.

Rule of 72

A shortcut for compounding: divide 72 by an annual rate to estimate how many years a balance takes to double. At 12%, roughly six years.

Fixed rate

An interest rate locked for an agreed period. Payments are predictable; you are protected from rises and excluded from falls, and breaking the arrangement early usually carries a fee.

Variable rate

An interest rate that can change during the term, following market rates or the lender's decisions. Payments can rise or fall.

Minimum payment

The smallest amount a lender will accept on a credit card in a given month, typically a small percentage of the balance. Because it shrinks as the balance falls, paying only the minimum extends a debt for years.

Balance transfer

Moving a credit card balance to another card, usually to obtain a lower promotional rate. Worth checking the transfer fee and the rate the balance reverts to when the promotion ends.

Secured and unsecured debt

Secured debt is backed by an asset the lender can take if you do not pay — a mortgage against a house, a loan against a car. Unsecured debt, such as a credit card, is not, which is why it usually carries a higher rate.

Overdraft

A facility allowing an account to go below zero up to an agreed limit. Convenient and typically expensive, especially when used continuously rather than briefly.

Budgeting

Budget

A prediction of where money will go over a period, recorded in advance so it can be compared against what actually happened. Useful because of the gap it reveals, not because of the limits it sets.

Fixed costs

Spending that is the same each period and already committed — rent, loan payments, insurance direct debits. Not really budget decisions in the short term.

Variable costs

Spending that changes with behaviour, such as groceries, fuel and eating out. The only part of a budget that can genuinely be steered month to month.

Irregular costs

Predictable costs arriving less often than monthly — annual insurance, motor tax, Christmas. The most common cause of a budget collapsing, because they are invisible in a monthly view.

Sinking fund

Money set aside monthly for a known future cost, so that when the bill arrives it is already paid for. Annual total divided by twelve, held separately.

Emergency fund

Accessible savings held to cover unexpected events such as job loss or a major repair, sized against essential spending rather than total spending.

Median

The middle value in a sorted list. Preferred to the average for budgeting because one extreme month does not distort it.

Pace

How far through a budget period you are, compared with how much of the budget you have used. Spending half a monthly budget by the 8th is a warning; by the 15th it is exactly on pace.

Safe-to-spend

The amount available per remaining day once committed costs and savings are taken out. More actionable than a remaining monthly total, because it can be acted on mid-month.

Zero-based budget

A method where every unit of income is assigned a job — spending, saving or debt — until nothing is unallocated. Thorough, and demanding to maintain.

Lifestyle creep

The tendency for spending to rise to absorb a rise in income, leaving no improvement in savings despite earning more.

Accounts and payments

Direct debit

Permission for a company to collect varying amounts from your account on agreed dates. The company controls the amount, which is why an incorrect bill leaves before you can dispute it, though schemes generally provide a right to an immediate refund.

Standing order

An instruction you give your bank to send a fixed amount to a named account on a schedule. You control it and you cancel it.

Available balance and cleared balance

The available balance includes pending transactions and any overdraft; the cleared balance is money actually settled. They can differ by several days and several hundred euro.

Pending transaction

A payment authorised but not yet settled. It reduces available funds and may still change in amount, which is common with fuel stations and hotels.

Reconciliation

Checking that a set of recorded transactions agrees with an independent record — typically walking a statement's running balance to confirm every row was read correctly.

Transfer

Money moved between accounts you own. It is neither income nor spending, and counting it as either inflates both sides of your finances.

Opening balance

The balance of an account before the first transaction you have a record of. Without it, statement data shows movement but not position.

Chargeback

A reversal of a card payment obtained through your card provider, typically where goods did not arrive or the transaction was not authorised. Distinct from a refund from the merchant.

Wealth

Net worth

Everything you own minus everything you owe, at a single point in time. The trend over months matters considerably more than the figure itself.

Asset

Something you own that has value — cash, savings, investments, a pension, property, a vehicle.

Liability

Something you owe — a mortgage, loan, credit card balance or overdraft.

Liquidity

How quickly an asset can be turned into spendable money without losing value. A current account is highly liquid; a house is not.

Depreciation

The loss in value of an asset over time, most visibly with vehicles and electronics.

Capital gain

The profit made when an asset is sold for more than it cost. Frequently taxable, with rules and rates varying by country and asset type.

Dividend

A share of a company's profits paid to shareholders, usually periodically and usually taxable.

Diversification

Spreading money across different assets so that a poor outcome in one does not determine the whole result.

Inflation

A general rise in prices over time, which reduces what a fixed amount of money buys. Savings earning less than inflation lose purchasing power even as the balance grows.

Real and nominal

Nominal figures are the plain numbers; real figures are adjusted for inflation. A 3% return with 4% inflation is a nominal gain and a real loss.
