# What an interest rate actually costs you

An interest rate is an abstraction; euro per month is not. Learn one piece of arithmetic — `balance × rate ÷ 12` — and every borrowing decision you ever make becomes concrete.

## Turn the percentage into euro

The one calculation worth memorising:

> **monthly interest ≈ balance × annual rate ÷ 12**

A €3,000 balance at 19.9%: `3000 × 0.199 ÷ 12` ≈ **€50 a month**.

That is the rent on the money. It buys nothing, reduces nothing and recurs every month the balance exists. Fifty euro a month is a phone bill, and phrased that way most people react to it very differently than they do to "19.9%".

Do this conversion for every debt you hold. It takes two minutes and it reorders priorities more reliably than any advice does.

## What APR actually means

**APR** — annual percentage rate — is the interest rate plus the compulsory fees, expressed as one annual figure. It exists so two products can be compared honestly, since a low rate with a large arrangement fee can cost more than a higher rate with none.

Three cautions:

- APR assumes the loan runs its stated term. Pay it off early and the effective cost differs.
- Advertised rates are often "representative" — a proportion of successful applicants get that rate, and you may be offered another.
- For credit cards, APR assumes a constant balance, which is not how anyone uses a credit card.

For savings you will see **AER** instead, which does the equivalent job: the annual rate with compounding accounted for, so accounts paying monthly and annually can be compared.

## Why minimum payments last for decades

Minimum payments are typically a small percentage of the balance, often with a floor. As the balance falls, so does the payment — which is why the end never arrives.

> €4,000 at 19.9%, minimum 2.5% (floor €5).
> Month one: minimum ≈ €100, interest ≈ €66. About €34 comes off the balance.
> Month two: the balance is €3,966, so the minimum drops to €99.
> The payment shrinks alongside the debt, and the tail stretches out for years.

Paying a **fixed** amount instead — even the same €100, held constant — changes the shape entirely, because every euro of reduced interest goes into the principal rather than into a smaller bill. This single change, with no extra money at all, typically removes years from a credit card balance.

## Compounding, in both directions

Compounding means interest is charged on interest already added. Left alone, a balance grows at an accelerating rate.

The rough guide is the **rule of 72**: divide 72 by the rate to get the years for a balance to double.

| Rate | Doubles in |
|---|---|
| 3% | ~24 years |
| 7% | ~10 years |
| 12% | ~6 years |
| 20% | ~3.5 years |

An untouched credit card balance at 20% roughly doubles in three and a half years. That is the same mathematics that makes long-term investing work, running against you instead of for you — which is precisely why clearing high-rate debt is often the highest-certainty return available to a household. You cannot reliably earn 20%; you can reliably stop paying it.

## Fixed and variable

A **fixed** rate is locked for a period — payments are predictable, and you are protected from rises and excluded from falls. A **variable** rate moves with the market or the lender's decisions.

Two practical points. First, know your fixed period's *end date*: rates often revert to a materially higher variable rate, and the reversion arrives as an unwelcome surprise for people who did not diarise it. Second, breaking a fixed rate early usually costs a fee.

## The number nobody calculates

Add up the interest you paid across every debt last year.

Most people have never seen this figure. It is available: for each debt, roughly `average balance × rate`. Total it.

It is frequently between €800 and €3,000 for a household with a couple of cards and a loan, and it is the truest measure of what borrowing is costing you — far more informative than any individual rate, because it is denominated in the units your life actually runs on.

## When borrowing is reasonable

None of this makes debt wrong. It makes it a purchase with a price.

Borrowing at 3% for something appreciating or essential is a different proposition from borrowing at 22% for something consumed in a weekend. The rate, the term and what you get in exchange are all part of one decision — and knowing the euro-per-month cost is what lets you make it deliberately rather than by default.
