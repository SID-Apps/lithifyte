How to find the money leaking out of your account

Updated 9 August 2026 · 4 min read

Most wasted money is not spent, it is leaked — recurring charges nobody decided to keep paying. An hour with your statements and a systematic method will usually surface between €40 and €150 a month, permanently.

On this page

  • The method
  • The five common leaks

Why leaks are different from spending

Spending is a decision. A leak is the absence of one — a charge that continues because cancelling it requires action and continuing requires none.

That asymmetry is deliberate on the seller's side, and it is why leaks are worth attacking first: fixing one takes ten minutes and never needs to be done again, whereas cutting discretionary spending requires a decision every week for the rest of your life. A €12 monthly subscription you no longer use is €144 a year, and the effort to stop it is a single afternoon.

The method

Step 1 — List every recurring payment. Sort twelve months of transactions by merchant and look for anything appearing monthly, quarterly or annually. Do not rely on memory; the whole nature of a leak is that you have forgotten it.

Check every account, including credit cards and any card attached to a wallet. Leaks hide on the account you look at least.

Step 2 — Score each one honestly. For every recurring charge, ask: when did I last actually use this? Not could I use it. Did I.

VerdictAction
Used weekly, glad to have itKeep, and stop feeling guilty about it
Used monthly-ishKeep, but check the tier — you may be paying for more than you use
Cannot remember the last timeCancel. You can always resubscribe, and you almost never will
Did not know it existedCancel today

Step 3 — Look for the five specific patterns below. These account for most of what people find.

The five common leaks

Forgotten trials. A trial you signed up for and did not cancel. These are typically small enough to slip under the threshold at which you would notice a line on a statement, which is precisely the design.

Price creep. The subscription you agreed to at €7.99 is now €13.99. Nobody ever gets a bill saying "this is 75% more than when you signed up" — you would notice that. It rises a euro at a time. Compare what you pay now against what you agreed to; a year-over-year comparison of the same merchant surfaces this instantly.

Duplicate capability. Two cloud storage plans. Three music services across a household. A gym membership and a fitness app. Each was reasonable when bought; together they are one service being paid for several times.

Zombie insurance and warranties. Cover for a phone you no longer own, an appliance long since replaced, travel insurance on a card you also hold as a standalone policy. Insurance is bought once and cancelled almost never.

Loyalty penalties. Broadband, energy, car and home insurance and mobile plans are frequently priced so that existing customers pay more than new ones. This is the largest single leak for many households, and often the least examined, because nothing on the statement changed — that is the point. An annual renewal you accept without shopping around is a decision to pay the loyalty penalty.

The annual review that catches the big ones

Monthly subscriptions are visible because they appear twelve times. Annual charges appear once, which makes them dramatically easier to miss and usually much larger.

Once a year, list every charge that appeared exactly once in twelve months and is over €50. Domain names, professional memberships, insurance renewals, annual software, warranties, club fees. Ask the same question about each: did I use this?

Doing this in a tool rather than by hand

If you have your transactions in something that can group by merchant, the whole exercise takes minutes instead of an hour:

  • Group by merchant, sorted by count. Anything appearing 12, 4 or 2 times a year is recurring by definition.
  • Compare the same merchant year over year. Price creep becomes obvious the moment you see the two figures side by side.
  • Flag statistical spikes. A category that is usually €40 and is suddenly €90 is either a price change or a duplicate, and both are worth ten seconds of attention.
  • Watch for the disappearance of a charge you still want. Leaks run both directions — a lapsed policy you meant to keep is worth catching too.

What to expect

For a typical household, an hour of this surfaces somewhere between €40 and €150 a month of genuinely unwanted spending. At €80 a month that is €960 a year, recovered permanently, for one afternoon of work — a better hourly rate than almost anything else available in personal finance.

Do it once a year. Put it in the calendar near a renewal date so it has an anchor, and it will keep paying.
