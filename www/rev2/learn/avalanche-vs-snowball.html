Avalanche vs snowball — which pays off debt faster?

Updated 9 August 2026 · 4 min read

Avalanche (highest interest rate first) always costs less. Snowball (smallest balance first) always finishes a debt sooner. The gap between them is usually smaller than people expect, which is why the one you will actually stick to is generally the right answer.

The setup

Both methods assume the same two things: you pay the minimum on every debt, and you have some extra amount each month. The only question is where the extra goes.

  • Avalanche — extra goes to the debt with the highest interest rate.
  • Snowball — extra goes to the debt with the smallest balance.

When a debt clears, its payment rolls into the next one. Payments grow as debts fall, which is where the momentum in both methods comes from.

Worked with real numbers

Four debts, and €400 a month spare after minimums:

DebtBalanceRateMinimum
Store card€90022.9%€30
Credit card€4,20019.9%€105
Car loan€8,5007.4%€190
Credit union loan€3,0008.5%€95

Avalanche order: store card (22.9%), credit card (19.9%), credit union (8.5%), car loan (7.4%).

Snowball order: store card (€900), credit union (€3,000), credit card (€4,200), car loan (€8,500).

Both start with the store card, which is common — the smallest debt is frequently also the most expensive. After that they diverge, and the difference over the full payoff comes to roughly a few hundred euro in favour of avalanche, and around a month or two of time.

That is the honest scale of it. Avalanche wins, and it wins by less than the internet arguments would suggest.

When the difference is genuinely large

The gap widens when the rate spread is wide and the expensive debt has a large balance. A €12,000 credit card at 21% alongside a €900 loan at 4% is a case where paying the small one first is expensive — every month you delay costs about €210 in interest on the card while you clear a debt costing €3.

Rule of thumb: if the highest-rate debt is also one of the largest, the arithmetic matters and avalanche is worth the discipline. If your debts are similar in rate, or the expensive one is small, pick whichever you will maintain.

Why snowball works for people anyway

The snowball's advantage is not financial, and pretending otherwise is why people bounce off avalanche and stop paying anything extra at all.

Clearing a debt entirely is a discrete, visible event. It removes a payment, a login, a statement and a small monthly reminder of the situation. Four debts becoming three feels like progress in a way that reducing a large balance by 4% does not — even though the second may be worth more money.

A plan you follow for three years beats an optimal plan you abandon in month five. If you have started and stopped before, that is real evidence about which method suits you, and it should outweigh a few hundred euro of theoretical saving.

The hybrid most people actually want

  1. Clear anything under about €500 first, regardless of rate. It is quick, it removes a payment, and the interest cost of the detour is negligible.
  2. Then go strictly by interest rate, highest first.
  3. Keep every cleared debt's payment in the pot. This is the part that does the heavy lifting in both methods, and it is the part people quietly skip.

The check that matters more than the order

Before optimising the order, confirm each payment actually covers its interest.

Monthly interest is roughly balance × annual rate ÷ 12. A €4,200 balance at 19.9% accrues about €70 a month. If the minimum is €105, only €35 is reducing the debt — and if you add €50 of new spending to that card, the balance is growing while you make every payment on time.

That situation is invisible from a statement that says "payment received, thank you", and it is how balances survive for a decade. Check it for every debt before deciding on any strategy. If a payment does not clear the interest, the order of payoff is not your problem.

Two things that beat both methods

Lower the rate. Moving a balance to a lower-rate product — a credit union loan, a genuine balance transfer offer — reduces the cost of every euro of the debt at once. Read the terms: an introductory rate that reverts, or a transfer fee that exceeds the interest saved, undoes the benefit. Whether any specific product suits you is a question for you and, if you want it, a regulated adviser.

Stop adding to it. No payoff method survives continued borrowing on the same card. If the balance is not falling despite payments, the leak is upstream of the strategy, and a month of tracking where the money goes will be worth more than any reordering.
