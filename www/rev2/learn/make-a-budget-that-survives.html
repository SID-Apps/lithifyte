How to build a budget that survives real life

Updated 9 August 2026 · 4 min read

Most budgets fail for structural reasons, not moral ones: the numbers were invented, annual bills were ignored, and progress was measured against a monthly total rather than the calendar. Fix those three things and a budget stops being an act of willpower.

Step 1 — Get twelve months of history

Not three. Twelve.

Three months of data will produce a budget that is confident and wrong, because it cannot see your car insurance, your Christmas, your holiday, the dental bill or the annual subscription that renews in March. A year of history contains every one of those at least once.

Download CSV statements for every account you use — current accounts, credit cards, the savings account you occasionally raid. If you have to do it one quarter at a time, do it; this is a one-off half hour that determines whether everything afterwards is based on reality.

Step 2 — Separate the transfers out

Before you total anything, find the money that moved between your own accounts.

If you move €600 to savings each month and count it as spending, and count the €600 arriving as income, you have invented €1,200 of activity that never happened. Your income looks inflated, your spending looks inflated, and your savings rate becomes meaningless.

Every transfer has two sides. Match them and exclude the pair.

Step 3 — Group by what things are, not what they are called

Bank descriptions are hostile. One shop appears as TESCO STORES 3184, TESCO EXPRESS, and TESCO-STORES DUBLIN; a subscription appears under a payment processor's name rather than the service you actually pay for.

Fold those variants together, then group into categories that are decision-shaped — categories you could actually act on. "Groceries" is a decision. "Card payment" is not. Aim for somewhere between eight and fifteen; below that everything is a blur, above that you spend your life sorting instead of deciding. More on this in categorising spending.

Step 4 — Take the median, not the average

For each category, line up the twelve monthly totals and take the middle one.

An average is dragged upwards by your worst month, which means you budget for a month that essentially never happens, underspend continuously, and conclude the budget is pointless. The median describes a normal month — and the outliers you excluded are not lost, they are the thing you are about to handle properly in step 5.

Step 5 — Pull the irregular bills out into a sinking fund

This is the structural fix that saves most budgets.

Add up everything that arrives less often than monthly: insurance, motor tax, the boiler service, professional fees, Christmas, birthdays, holidays. Divide by twelve. That figure is a monthly bill, even in the eleven months nothing is due.

Say it comes to €2,760 a year, so €230 a month. Move €230 into a separate account each month and treat it as spent. When the €640 insurance bill arrives, it is not an emergency, a credit card balance, or a sign you are bad with money. It is a withdrawal from the account that exists for it. See sinking funds.

Step 6 — Commit the fixed costs from day one

Add up rent or mortgage, loan payments, utilities and everything else that leaves automatically. Subtract that from income immediately, along with the sinking-fund contribution and any savings you want treated as non-negotiable.

What remains is the only money that any budget can genuinely steer. It is usually far smaller than people expect, and knowing the true figure is worth more than a dozen resolutions about coffee.

Step 7 — Measure against the calendar, not the total

Here is where budgets are usually lost.

You have €520 for groceries. On the 12th you have spent €260. Half the budget — but you are only 39% of the way through the month, so you are running about 28% hot. Left as "€260 remaining" that fact is invisible until it is too late to act on it.

The useful number is what is left per remaining day:

€520 budget − €260 spent = €260 left, over 19 remaining days = €13.70 a day, against your normal €17.30. You are not in trouble. You are on a slightly tight run for two weeks, and you know it on the 12th rather than the 28th.

Anything that gives you a running per-day figure will beat a monthly bar, because a monthly bar only tells you the truth on the last day of the month, by which point it is history rather than a decision.

Step 8 — Review monthly, adjust quarterly

Once a month, spend ten minutes on three questions: what went over, was it a wrong estimate or a genuine one-off, and does anything need changing?

Write the answer down next to the month it happened in. In six months you will be looking at a spike with no memory of what caused it, and "visitors for two weeks" is the difference between correctly ignoring it and wrongly concluding your grocery spending has permanently risen.

Change the actual numbers about every three months. Monthly tinkering means you never find out whether a figure was realistic; annual review means you spend most of the year budgeting against a life you no longer lead.

The two-minute version

  1. Twelve months of statements from every account.
  2. Match and remove transfers between your own accounts.
  3. Fold merchant name variants together; use eight to fifteen decision-shaped categories.
  4. Median, not average.
  5. Annual bills ÷ 12 into a sinking fund, treated as a monthly bill.
  6. Subtract fixed costs and savings first.
  7. Track per remaining day, not per month.
  8. Review monthly, adjust quarterly, and write down why the odd months were odd.
