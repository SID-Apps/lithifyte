# Net worth, explained without the jargon

Source: https://lithifyte.com/learn/net-worth
Updated: 2026-08-09

Net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe. It is the only single number that captures your whole financial position — and its value lies almost entirely in watching it move, not in what it says today.

## The definition

**Net worth = everything you own − everything you owe.**

That is the whole concept. It is a snapshot of your financial position at one moment, and it is the only figure that captures the whole picture — income tells you about flow, savings tell you about one pot, net worth tells you where you actually stand.

## Calculating yours

**What you own (assets)**

| Asset | What value to use |
|---|---|
| Current and savings accounts | Today's balance |
| Cash | Today's amount |
| Pensions | Current statement value |
| Investments, funds, shares | Current market value |
| Crypto | Current market value |
| Property | A realistic sale price, not what you hope for |
| Vehicles | Trade-in value, not the price you paid |
| Valuables | Only if genuinely saleable and material |

**What you owe (liabilities)**

| Liability | What value to use |
|---|---|
| Mortgage | Outstanding balance |
| Loans | Outstanding balance |
| Credit cards | Current balance, including this month's |
| Overdraft | Amount currently used |
| Family loans | The full amount, uncomfortable as it is |
| Tax owed | If due and not yet paid |

Subtract the second total from the first. Fifteen minutes, once a month.

## Two valuation rules that keep it honest

**Value assets at what you could actually get, not what you paid or hope for.** A car is worth its trade-in price. A house is worth what comparable properties nearby actually sold for. Sentimental value is real and belongs nowhere near this calculation.

**Do not double-count.** If you hold €5,000 in a savings account and have earmarked it as your "house deposit fund", it is one €5,000, not two entries. This sounds obvious and is a very common error once people start keeping goal pots alongside account balances.

## A negative net worth is normal, not a verdict

A recent graduate with student debt, a new mortgage holder, someone early in a car loan — all frequently negative, and none of it says anything about how well they are managing money.

Net worth is a position, not a grade. Someone at −€15,000 improving by €700 a month is in a considerably better place than someone at €40,000 sliding by €300 a month, and only the trend reveals which is which.

## The trend is the whole point

A single net worth figure is nearly useless. The same figure recorded monthly for a year is one of the most informative things you can have.

It answers the question that individual accounts cannot: **am I actually getting anywhere?** You can save diligently into one account while a credit card grows faster elsewhere. Net worth catches that, and nothing else does, because it is the only measure that sees both sides at once.

Record it on the same day each month — the 1st is easy to remember. Do not adjust it, smooth it, or skip bad months. A year of honest monthly figures will show you your real trajectory, and the shape of that line tends to be more motivating than any budget.

## Reading the movement

Your net worth changes for exactly four reasons, and they are worth separating:

1. **You saved or spent.** The part you control directly.
2. **You paid down debt.** Every principal payment increases net worth euro for euro — which is why debt repayment is a form of saving, even though it does not feel like one.
3. **Asset values moved.** Markets and property prices, entirely outside your control.
4. **You bought or sold something large.** Usually neutral at the moment of transaction — €20,000 of cash becomes a €20,000 car — and then negative as the car depreciates.

When your net worth jumps, check which of these caused it. A rise driven entirely by a property revaluation is genuine but not repeatable, and mistaking it for progress you made is how people conclude they can afford more than they can.

## Staleness, and the trap in it

Property and vehicle valuations are estimates that decay. A house value entered two years ago and never revisited quietly makes every subsequent figure fiction.

Either update the estimate at least annually, or mark it as stale so you know which part of the number you should not trust. The dangerous version is the one that looks precise and is not — a net worth carried by an optimistic property estimate from three years ago, mistaken for a measurement.

## What it is not for

It is not a comparison with other people. Ages, careers, countries, family situations and inheritances differ so completely that the exercise carries no information.

The only useful comparison is with your own figure from last year. That one tells you something true.
