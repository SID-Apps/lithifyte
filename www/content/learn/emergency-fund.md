## What it is for

An emergency fund exists to convert a disaster into an inconvenience.

It is not a savings goal, not an investment, and not a pot for things you knew were coming. Its entire job is to absorb the events that would otherwise force you into expensive borrowing — a lost job, an illness, a car that dies, a boiler in February.

The test of an emergency fund is not how large it is. It is whether an unwelcome surprise makes you rearrange a weekend or take on debt at 19%.

## Why "three to six months" is a bad starting point

You will hear this everywhere, and it is not wrong so much as unhelpfully vague. Three to six months of *what*, exactly?

Most people hear "months of income" or "months of spending" — including holidays, restaurants and subscriptions. That produces an enormous target, which feels unreachable, which is a common reason people never start at all.

An emergency fund only ever has to cover what you would **actually** spend in a bad month. In a genuine crisis you are not going out, not booking flights, and cancelling anything cancellable. The right base is your **essential** monthly spending.

## Work out your own number

**Step 1 — Find your essential monthly total.** Go through your categories and keep only what continues in a bad month: housing, utilities, groceries, transport to look for work, insurance, minimum debt payments, childcare, medication. Drop eating out, subscriptions you could pause, clothes, gifts, holidays.

For many households the essential figure is 50–65% of normal spending. That alone makes the target dramatically less frightening.

**Step 2 — Decide how many months.** Not a rule — a judgement about your own situation:

| Your circumstances | Reasonable range |
|---|---|
| Two incomes, secure employment, no dependants | 3 months |
| One income, or dependants, or a mortgage | 4–6 months |
| Self-employed, contract work, irregular income | 6–9 months |
| Specialised role where a job search takes longer | 9–12 months |

The real question behind the multiplier is: *how long would it realistically take me to replace this income?* Someone in a common role in a city answers differently from someone in a niche role in a small town.

**Step 3 — Multiply.** Essential monthly spend × months. That is your target.

> Example: essentials of €2,100 a month, two incomes, secure work → 3 months → **€6,300**. Not €18,000, which is what "six months of spending" would have suggested from a €3,000 monthly outgoing.

## Where to keep it

Two properties matter and they are in tension with almost everything else you might do with money.

**You must be able to reach it within a day or two.** An emergency fund locked in a 90-day notice account is not an emergency fund. Neither is one invested in the stock market, where the moment you are most likely to need it — a recession — is precisely when its value is most likely to be down.

**It should be separate from your current account.** Money that sits alongside your everyday balance gets spent without a decision ever being made. A separate account, ideally at a different institution, adds a deliberate step, and that friction is the point.

Beyond those two, take whatever interest you can get. Interest is a bonus here, not the objective.

## Building it without heroics

Start with **one month of essentials**. That single month covers the overwhelming majority of real-life surprises — the car, the appliance, the vet, the excess on a claim — and it converts the most common financial shocks from crises into annoyances. It is also reachable, which matters more than optimality.

Then let it grow: automate a standing order the day after payday so the decision is made once rather than monthly, and put windfalls — a refund, a bonus, a rebate — straight in.

If you are carrying high-interest debt, most people are better served by holding one month of essentials, clearing the expensive debt, and then building the rest. Paying 19% interest to hold cash earning 2% is an expensive kind of safety. That is arithmetic, not advice; your own tolerance for risk is yours to weigh.

## When you use it, you have not failed

Using an emergency fund is the fund working. That is the whole design.

The only thing to do afterwards is start refilling it, at whatever rate is realistic, and to note what the emergency actually was. After a few years you will know your own pattern of surprises — and if the same category keeps appearing, it was never an emergency at all. It was an irregular cost that belongs in a [sinking fund](/learn/sinking-funds).
