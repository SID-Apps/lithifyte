## Start with the real target

The deposit is not the amount you need. It is the largest part of the amount you need, which is a different thing, and the gap between the two is where first-time buyers get caught.

A realistic target is the deposit **plus** the costs of buying:

| Cost | Typical shape |
|---|---|
| Deposit | A percentage of the purchase price — commonly 10% for a first home, more for a second or for an investment property |
| Legal fees | A fixed fee plus outlays |
| Valuation and survey | Modest but non-optional; a survey is worth far more than it costs |
| Stamp duty | A percentage of the price, varying by country and price band |
| Mortgage protection and home insurance | Required before drawdown |
| Moving and immediate essentials | Consistently underestimated — furniture, appliances, the first bills |

Percentages and duties change and differ by country, so take the current figures from your own lender or a current official source rather than from any article, including this one. The point is structural: **budget for the deposit plus roughly a further slice on top**, and find out what that slice actually is where you live before you set a target.

## Then find your true monthly saving

Not what you intend to save. What actually accumulated.

Take the balance of your savings twelve months ago and the balance today, and divide the difference by twelve. That number includes the months you dipped into it, the months you did not get round to transferring, and the months something went wrong — which is precisely why it is the honest figure and your intended amount is not.

> Intended: €1,000 a month. Actual over twelve months: €7,800, so **€650 a month**. Against a €40,000 target, that is the difference between a 40-month plan and a 62-month one — over eighteen months of difference, invisible until you measure it.

## Divide, and get a date

Target minus what you already have, divided by your true monthly saving, equals months to go.

A date is worth far more than a target, for a reason that is entirely psychological: a target is a wish, and a date is a plan you can test. It also converts every decision into a comparison you can actually make. Spending €2,000 on a holiday is not "€2,000". It is "three months later", and only one of those two framings lets you decide honestly. Sometimes three months later is entirely worth it — but now it is a choice rather than a drift.

## Closing the gap

Four levers, in descending order of how much they usually move the date:

1. **Housing costs now.** Rent is the largest single obstacle to saving for a home, and it is the one nobody wants to look at. A shared arrangement or a cheaper area for eighteen months moves the date more than every other change combined.
2. **The gap between intended and actual.** Automate the transfer for the day after payday. Most of the shortfall is not overspending; it is money that stayed in the current account long enough to be absorbed.
3. **Windfalls.** Bonuses, tax refunds, gifts. These are the largest lump sums most people ever handle, and they are the easiest to dissolve without noticing.
4. **Category trimming.** Real, but usually smaller than expected. Cutting €150 a month from spending is genuinely hard work and moves a €40,000 target by about five months.

## Two things not to do

**Do not raid your emergency fund for the deposit.** Buying a house is precisely when you most need a buffer — the first year of ownership generates costs you cannot predict, and moving in with zero cushion means the first broken appliance goes on a credit card.

**Do not invest a deposit you need within five years.** Markets do not care about your timeline. Money you will need on a known, near date belongs somewhere boring and accessible, and the interest you forgo is the price of not having your deposit fall 20% in the quarter you were going to bid.

## Track it monthly, honestly

Once a month, record what you have, what the target is, and what the implied date now says.

The date will move. That is not failure; that is the plan responding to reality, which is the only thing a plan is good for. What matters is that it moves for reasons you can name — a bad month, a lender's requirements changing, a target revised because you looked at actual listings — rather than drifting silently for a year until you check and find you are nowhere near.
