## The test a category has to pass

A category is useful if knowing its total could change something you do.

"Groceries" passes: if it is €700 a month you might shop differently. "Subscriptions" passes: you can cancel one this afternoon. "Card payments" fails — it describes the mechanism, not the choice, and no decision follows from knowing the number. "Miscellaneous" fails hardest of all, because it grows to absorb everything you did not want to think about, and by month four it is the second-largest category on the list.

## How many

Between eight and fifteen for most households.

Fewer than eight and everything blurs — a single "Living" category tells you nothing you did not already know. More than about fifteen and two things go wrong: individual categories get too small to be worth acting on, and sorting becomes a chore, at which point you stop doing it and the whole exercise dies quietly.

A workable starting set:

| Category | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Housing | Rent or mortgage, management fees, property tax |
| Utilities | Electricity, gas, water, broadband, phone |
| Groceries | Supermarket food and household basics |
| Transport | Fuel, public transport, tax, insurance, servicing |
| Eating out | Restaurants, takeaway, coffee, pub |
| Health | Doctor, dentist, pharmacy, health insurance |
| Subscriptions | Streaming, apps, gym, memberships |
| Shopping | Clothes, homeware, gifts, electronics |
| Family | Childcare, school, activities, pets |
| Debt payments | Loan and credit card repayments |
| Savings and investing | Money moved out of reach on purpose |
| Irregular | Annual and occasional bills — see [sinking funds](/learn/sinking-funds) |

Adjust it to your life. A category you never use is clutter; a category you find yourself constantly wanting to split is two categories.

## Split by decision, not by shop

The instinct is to categorise by merchant, which breaks the moment a shop sells several things. A supermarket sells groceries, wine, a phone charger and petrol. A department store sells clothes and a birthday present.

The question to ask is not *where did I buy it* but *what decision does it belong to*. If cutting back on alcohol is a thing you might do, alcohol needs to be visible rather than buried inside groceries. If it is not, do not create the category — you would be doing bookkeeping for a decision you are never going to make.

## Automate it or abandon it

Nobody sorts transactions by hand for a year. Everybody intends to.

The way out is rules: a pattern matched against the description, mapping to a category. `TESCO` → Groceries. `ESB` → Utilities. `NETFLIX` → Subscriptions. Do it once and every future transaction from that merchant sorts itself.

Two refinements make rules considerably more useful:

- **Wildcards**, so `AIB*` catches every variant a bank emits without you writing six rules.
- **Amount ranges**, so the same merchant can be Groceries under €150 and Shopping above it — genuinely useful for shops where the weekly food run and the occasional appliance both happen.

And you need an escape hatch: a per-transaction override that beats the rule, so a single exception does not force you to weaken a rule that is right 95% of the time.

## The 80/20 of it

Roughly 80% of your transactions come from roughly 20% of your merchants. Fifteen or twenty rules covering your regular shops, utilities and subscriptions will usually sort the large majority of your spending automatically.

The remainder is a long tail of one-off purchases, and it is genuinely fine to leave much of that as "Uncategorised" — as long as you look at the total. If uncategorised is 4% of spending, ignore it. If it is 25%, your rules are not doing their job and every other number on the page is unreliable.

## Two things to keep out of your spending totals

**Transfers between your own accounts.** Moving €500 to savings is not spending. Counted as such it inflates your outgoings and, on the other side, your income. Pair both sides and exclude them.

**Refunds and reversals.** A €120 refund is not €120 of income; it is a €120 purchase that unhappened. Netting it against the original category keeps the total honest. Left as income, it quietly flatters your month.

## Categorise for your future self

The most useful thing you can add is context, and it costs a sentence.

When something unusual happens — a month where groceries collapse, a category that doubles — write down why, attached to that month. *"Two weeks away"*, *"used a voucher"*, *"bought a year of it upfront"*.

In eight months you will be looking at that dip with no memory at all, and without the note you will draw a confident wrong conclusion about your habits. A number tells you what happened. Only you can record why, and only at the time.
