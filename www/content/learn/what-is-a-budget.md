## The definition most people carry around

Ask someone what a budget is and you will usually get something like *"a limit on what I'm allowed to spend"*. Under that definition, going over is a failure, a moral one, and the natural response to failing is to stop looking. Which is exactly what happens: most abandoned budgets are abandoned in the second or third month, shortly after the first overspend.

## A more useful definition

A budget is a **forecast of where your money will go**, written down in advance so that you can compare it against what actually happened.

That is all. It is a hypothesis. And the value is not in obeying it — it is in the gap between the guess and the outcome, because that gap is information you cannot get any other way.

You budget €400 for groceries and spend €530. Under the first definition you failed. Under the second you have learned that your grocery spending is €530, which you did not know, and now you get to ask the interesting question: is €400 the wrong number, or was this month unusual?

Very often the answer is that €400 was never realistic. It was a wish dressed up as a plan.

## Where the numbers should come from

The single biggest improvement most people can make is to stop inventing budget figures and start deriving them.

Take your last six months of spending in a category and find the **median** — the middle value when you line the months up in order. Not the average: one Christmas or one car repair drags an average upwards and leaves you budgeting for a month that never occurs.

| Month | Groceries |
|---|---|
| February | €468 |
| March | €502 |
| April | €511 |
| May | €530 |
| June | €545 |
| July | €780 (visitors for two weeks) |

The average is €556. The median is €520. Budget €556 and you will underspend most months and feel vaguely as though the budget does nothing. Budget €520 and you have a number that describes a normal month honestly, with July correctly understood as an exception rather than folded into the baseline.

## Fixed, variable, and the ones that ambush you

Three different things get called "expenses" and they behave nothing alike.

**Fixed** — rent, loan payments, insurance direct debits. Same amount, same time, every month. These are not really budget decisions at all; they are already committed. The only useful question is whether they are too big a share of your income.

**Variable** — groceries, fuel, going out. These fluctuate with your behaviour, and they are the only part of a budget you can genuinely steer month to month.

**Irregular** — car insurance annually, the boiler service, a birthday cluster in October, the dentist. These are the ones that break budgets, because they are entirely predictable in a *year* and completely invisible in a *month*. A budget that only looks at monthly averages will be ambushed by them roughly four times a year, and each ambush feels like personal failure when it is simply arithmetic. [Sinking funds](/learn/sinking-funds) are the fix.

## Why the calendar matters more than the total

A monthly total tells you almost nothing on the 12th of the month. You have spent €260 of your €520 grocery budget: is that fine, or a problem?

It depends entirely on how far through the month you are. On the 15th, half of a monthly budget is exactly on pace. On the 8th, it is a warning. The useful figure is not *"how much is left"* but *"how much is left per remaining day, compared with what I normally spend per day"*.

That framing also solves the biggest single failure mode of monthly budgets: a large fixed bill leaving on the 2nd makes the whole month look catastrophic for the following twenty-eight days. Commit fixed bills from day one and they stop distorting the picture.

## What a budget is for

Three things, in order of how much they actually matter:

1. **Noticing.** Most overspending is not decided; it accumulates unobserved. Simply seeing the number changes behaviour before any rule does.
2. **Deciding in advance.** Choosing at the start of the month is easier than choosing in the shop, because in the shop you are deciding one purchase and at the start of the month you are deciding a pattern.
3. **Freeing up attention.** A working budget means you stop wondering whether you can afford things. That is the actual prize, and it is more valuable than any specific euro saved.

## When you go over

Ask which of these it was, because they call for different responses:

- **The estimate was wrong.** Fix the estimate. This is the most common answer and needs no willpower whatsoever.
- **It was a genuine one-off.** Note what it was, next to the month it happened in, and move on. Your future self will otherwise read the spike as a change in habit.
- **The pattern has changed.** Something has genuinely shifted — a new commute, a price rise. The budget has just done its job by detecting it.
- **You chose to.** Also fine. A budget you consciously override is still working; a budget you have stopped reading is not.
