## Why you cannot see them from inside

Each individual purchase feels like a decision you made for a reason. Zoom out to a year and you see something else entirely: a system with a rhythm, running largely without your input.

This is not a character flaw. Habits are what free your attention for other things. But it means the useful question is rarely *"why did I buy that?"* and almost always *"what shape does my spending have, and do I want that shape?"*

Four patterns cover most of what you will find.

## Pattern 1 — The weekly rhythm

Group a few months of spending by day of the week and a shape appears immediately. Most households have a heavy day (a big shop) and a heavy pair (Friday and Saturday), and the weekend can easily carry twice a weekday's spending.

**What to do with it.** Ignore the moralising interpretation; the useful part is practical. If your heavy day is Saturday, that is when a decision is actually being made and the only moment where changing anything is possible. And if a spending limit needs to survive, it has to accommodate the rhythm — a flat daily allowance will be broken every single weekend, which teaches you nothing except that limits do not work.

## Pattern 2 — The monthly cliff

Plot daily spending across the month. The overwhelmingly common shape: a large cliff in the first three days, a plateau, and a tail that either flattens or spikes in the last week.

The cliff is rent, mortgage and direct debits. The tail depends on the person — some tighten as the month ends, some spend more once they can see payday coming.

**What to do with it.** The cliff is why a naive monthly budget feels catastrophic for twenty-eight days a month. Fixed costs should be committed on day one and removed from the picture, so what you are watching is the part you can actually influence. And if the cliff lands before your salary does, that is a timing problem — often solved by moving one direct debit by a week.

## Pattern 3 — The season

Line up twelve months per category. Some are flat; some breathe.

- Electricity and heating rise in winter.
- Groceries spike around holidays and school breaks.
- Clothing clusters in two or three months, not twelve.
- Motor costs land in a lump — tax, insurance and testing frequently share a month.
- December is usually the largest month of the year by a wide margin, and January the tightest.

**What to do with it.** Stop comparing a month with the previous month, and compare it with the same month last year. December against November tells you nothing. December against last December tells you whether anything actually changed.

## Pattern 4 — The drift

The hardest to see and the most consequential.

Drift is slow, one-directional movement: groceries at €480 two years ago, €520 last year, €580 now. No single month looks wrong. Each increase is individually justifiable. Over three years it is €1,200 a year, arrived at without a decision ever being made.

Some drift is inflation and entirely outside your control. Some is lifestyle creep — the phenomenon where a rise in income is absorbed within a few months, leaving you no further ahead than before.

**How to see it.** Take a twelve-month rolling total per category and watch that line rather than the monthly one. Rolling totals remove seasonality completely, so a rising line means a genuine change in level, not a busy month. This is the single most valuable view in personal finance analysis, and almost nobody looks at it.

## Distinguishing a spike from a trend

A category doubles in a month. Panic or ignore?

Compare against the **median** and the **typical variation** of that category. If groceries usually run €500 and vary by about €40, a €700 month is unusual and worth explaining. If they usually run €500 and swing by €180 routinely, €700 is just Tuesday.

Then answer the question a number cannot: **why**. And write it down, attached to that month. *"Two birthdays and a communion"*, *"stocked the freezer"*, *"bought a year of it upfront"*.

Six months later that note is the difference between correctly ignoring a spike and wrongly concluding your habits have changed. This is the one piece of the analysis no software can do for you — the data records what, and only you were there for why.

## The five-minute monthly review

1. Total in, total out, difference. Is the difference the direction you expect?
2. Any category more than about 30% off its median? Name the reason.
3. Any new recurring payment? Was that deliberate?
4. Rolling twelve-month total on your two largest categories — flat, or drifting?
5. Write one sentence about anything odd, attached to the month.

Five minutes, twelve times a year, and you will know more about your own money than almost anyone you know.
