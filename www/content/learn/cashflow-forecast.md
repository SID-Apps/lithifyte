## Most of next month is already decided

People treat their finances as unpredictable, and then behave with impressive consistency. Rent leaves on the same day. The same four direct debits go out in the same week. Groceries land within a fairly narrow band, and the weekly shop happens on the same day it has for years.

Genuinely unpredictable spending is usually a small minority of the total. Forecasting is mostly a matter of adding up the parts that are already known, and being honest about the variance in what remains.

## The four ingredients

**1 — Opening balance.** What is in the account today. Obvious, and routinely omitted; a forecast of movements without a starting point tells you about flow but not about whether you run out.

**2 — Known income.** Salary dates and amounts. If your income is irregular, use a conservative figure — the lower end of your recent range, not the average, because a forecast that assumes a good month and gets a bad one fails in the direction that hurts.

**3 — Recurring outgoings.** Every standing order and direct debit, with its date. Get these from twelve months of statements rather than memory: quarterly and annual items are exactly what memory omits.

**4 — Variable spending.** The median of your recent months per category, not the average. Then convert to a daily rate, because timing matters as much as the total.

## Work it as a running balance

A single monthly total hides the problem. What matters is whether the balance stays above zero at every point in between, and the shape of a month is deeply uneven — rent on the 1st, salary on the 25th.

Lay it out by date:

| Date | Event | Amount | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Sep | Opening | | €1,840 |
| 1 Sep | Rent | −€1,200 | €640 |
| 3 Sep | Utilities | −€180 | €460 |
| 4–24 Sep | Variable, ~€58/day × 21 | −€1,218 | −€758 |
| 25 Sep | Salary | +€2,650 | €1,892 |

That forecast has found something a monthly total never would: the account goes negative around the 19th, even though the month as a whole is comfortably positive. That is a real problem with a cheap solution — move a direct debit — and it is entirely invisible without a running balance.

## Sizing the buffer honestly

Your variable spending will not equal its median. The question is by how much, and the answer is in your own history.

Take the last twelve months of variable spending, and find the typical distance of each month from the median. That typical distance is your volatility. Someone whose variable spending is €1,180, €1,220, €1,150, €1,240 needs a small buffer. Someone whose months run €700, €1,900, €1,100, €2,300 needs a large one, and no amount of averaging will make them predictable.

Using the *median distance from the median*, rather than a simple average, keeps one extreme month from inflating the buffer permanently. One catastrophic August should not make every forecast pessimistic for a year.

## Three mistakes that make forecasts useless

**Forgetting the annual bills.** A forecast built on monthly averages is blind to the €640 insurance renewal, and it will be wrong by exactly that amount in exactly that month. Handle these with a [sinking fund](/learn/sinking-funds) so they leave the forecast as a smooth monthly cost.

**Using averages that include outliers.** One holiday raises every subsequent month's projection, so the forecast is consistently pessimistic, so you stop believing it.

**Forecasting only one account.** If you move money between accounts, a single-account forecast is a forecast of transfers as much as of spending. Include everything you actually pay from.

## How far ahead is useful

**Two to four weeks** is the actionable horizon: enough time to move a bill, delay a purchase or move money across, and short enough to be genuinely reliable.

**Three months** is useful for planning — a large purchase, whether a goal date is realistic.

**Beyond six months**, a cashflow forecast becomes a scenario rather than a prediction. Still useful for asking "what if", but do not make commitments on it.

## Use it as a question machine

The value of a forecast is not the number. It is the ability to ask *what if* and get an answer in seconds.

What if I cut eating out by a third? What if the car insurance rises €150? What if I add €200 a month to the credit card? Each of these has a definite answer once the model exists, and each answer is a decision made with information instead of anxiety.

That is the actual product of forecasting: not certainty, but the removal of the vague background worry that comes from not knowing.
